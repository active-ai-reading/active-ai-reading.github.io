Where this paper comes from: a literature map

The four lines of work it builds on, and the one it argues with.

🔬 Research skills · Literature review
on Why Pretraining Fails to Share Cross-Lingual Knowledge
by Course team · October 16, 2026 · example post · edit this page

This paper sits where four lines of work meet. Knowing them makes it much easier to see what’s new.

1. The cross-lingual knowledge gap (the problem)

Multilingual models answer the same factual question differently depending on the language. Qi et al. measured this inconsistency directly (Qi et al., 2023), and ECLeKTic built a benchmark around knowledge that exists in one language’s sources and must be transferred to others (Goldman et al., 2025). Earlier, Chang et al. studied the “curse of multilinguality” at the level of language modeling across 250 languages (Chang et al., 2024). All of these observe finished models; none can say where the gap comes from. That’s the opening this paper takes.

2. Cloned languages as a laboratory (the method, part 1)

The trick of duplicating a vocabulary to create a “fake second language” goes back at least to K et al. (2020), who used it to ask what makes multilingual BERT cross-lingual (K et al., 2020). Schäfer et al. (2024) used cloned languages to study language imbalance (Schäfer et al., 2024), and Howe & Wingate (2026) to study compartmentalization in aggregate terms (Howe & Wingate, 2026). This paper’s addition is to measure knowledge in the clone setup, fact by fact.

3. Injecting fictive facts (the method, part 2)

To know exactly how often a model saw a fact, insert facts that cannot exist anywhere else. Physics of Language Models 3.1 made this standard for studying knowledge storage (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2024), and Chang et al. (2024) used it to trace knowledge acquisition during pretraining (Chang et al., 2024). (Why counting real facts is hard: (Elazar et al., 2024).) See also the fictive-facts explainer.

4. Tokens, scripts and vocabulary overlap (the mechanism)

Does sharing subwords matter for transfer? The classic answer was “surprisingly little”: Conneau et al. and Artetxe et al. found that cross-lingual structure emerges without any shared vocabulary (Conneau et al., 2020; Artetxe et al., 2020). More recently Kallini et al. found that overlap, especially overlap with matching meanings, does help (Kallini et al., 2025), and RomanSetu proposed romanizing text to unlock multilingual ability (J et al., 2024).

This paper reconciles the two camps: structure does emerge without shared tokens (their embedding analysis agrees with Conneau et al.), but knowledge does not follow it. And their transliteration control (−0.3%) explains why romanization helps mainly between related languages, where cognates supply shared meanings.

The one it argues with

PreAlign reports that cross-lingual knowledge gaps narrow with scale and that alignment emerges spontaneously in larger models (Li et al., 2024). This paper finds scale helps only a little (0.9% → 5.9% from 360M to 7B) and not at all in the clone setup, and attributes the disagreement to measuring a normalized ratio rather than absolute accuracy. Reading both papers side by side is a good exercise in how metric choices shape conclusions.

Bigger picture

The conclusion connects to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, the idea that models trained on different data and modalities converge toward similar representations (Huh et al., 2024). This paper is a caution: similar structure in two token spaces is not enough for knowledge to flow between them. And it points to tokenizer-free models such as ByT5 (Xue et al., 2022) as an untested way around the problem.
