Literature review: where spurious forgetting comes from

The paper sits where four lines of work meet: catastrophic forgetting, forgetting that is actually recoverable, factual recall as associative memory, and what training choices change how much is forgotten.

🔬 Research skills · Literature review
on When Forgetting is not Catastrophic: On the Mechanics of Spurious Forgetting
by Tomer Atia · November 17, 2026 · edit this page

I read the paper’s introduction, discussion and reference list, plus the arXiv abstracts of eight cited papers (the ones with links). For the rest I only know what the paper says about them. I ran no code.

The papers this builds on

The problem: catastrophic forgetting. Training on new data damages old knowledge: classic reports (McCloskey & Cohen 1989, French 1999), deep networks (Goodfellow et al. 2014, Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) and language models (Luo et al. 2025, Zucchet et al. 2025). The paper says part of this is something else.

The direct ancestors: a drop in performance is not a loss of knowledge.

  • Zheng et al. 2025 coined “spurious forgetting”: performance drops often reflect lost task alignment, linked to orthogonal weight updates. The new paper looks for a mechanism instead (a common shift of representations that normalization later withdraws) and only suggests it may be a concrete form of Zheng et al.’s task-alignment loss.
  • Kotha et al. 2024: fine-tuning skews which task the model infers from a prompt, and a changed prompt recovers some capability. The new paper’s recovery needs no special prompt, probe or replay.
  • De Lange et al. 2023, the “stability gap”: performance drops after a new task starts, then recovers. This is the same curve shape, but the paper cites it in one sentence only (my observation). The setting differs: they study continual-learning methods, the paper studies plain fine-tuning without replay.
  • Shallow forgetting (Murata et al. 2020, Davari et al. 2022, Hess et al. 2024, Lanzillotta et al. 2026): old information stays linearly decodable even when outputs fail. The paper’s “hidden, not erased” is this idea for individual facts.
  • Older roots, as the paper describes them: stored versus accessible memory in psychology (Tulving & Pearlstone 1966) and recovery of forgotten associations in connectionist networks (Hinton & Plaut 1987, Atkins & Murre 1998).

How a model stores a fact (the setup). Feed-forward layers as key-value memories (Geva et al. 2021); Meng et al. 2022 show facts are localized and editable with rank-one updates, and their CounterFact set supplies the old facts for the pretrained-model test. The controlled synthetic biographies come from Allen-Zhu & Li 2024. Zucchet et al. 2025 find that fine-tuning on new facts “quickly corrupts” existing ones, which is closely related to the question here (my reading, not stated by the paper).

What changes how much is forgotten. Larger learning rates mean more forgetting (Gupta et al. 2023, Ibrahim et al. 2024, Lin et al. 2026, Rofin et al. 2026), and Springer et al. 2025 show that longer-pretrained models can be harder to fine-tune. The paper finds its collapse deepens with the learning rate and concludes part of that effect is spurious.

The concepts it connects to

Concept Role in this paper
Catastrophic forgetting The phenomenon it splits in two
Availability vs. accessibility (psychology) The old idea behind “hidden, not erased”
Shallow vs. deep forgetting Evidence that old information survives in representations
Stability gap A forgetting curve of the same shape
Factual recall as associative memory The minimal model and the synthetic setup
Normalization layers Usually stabilizers; here they withdraw the shift and cause recovery
Shared structure is learned first (Saxe et al. 2019) Explains why the shift reverses only slowly
Rank-one weight edits The fix removes one direction of each update; the link to editing is my observation

Read first, and what I’m unsure about

Read Zheng et al. 2025 (the phenomenon), De Lange et al. 2023 (the same curve elsewhere) and Zucchet et al. 2025 (the data and dynamics).

Open questions: is the paper’s common shift the same thing as Zheng et al.’s orthogonal update (the paper only says “may be”)? Where does it stand relative to the stability gap? And of the 2026 references I checked only Lanzillotta et al.

Paper: arXiv:2610.08718.
