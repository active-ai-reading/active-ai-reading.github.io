Is nesting the cheapest way to build a model suite?

Five follow-ups that test the paper's efficiency claims, all starting from checkpoints the authors have already released.

🔬 Research skills · Follow-ups worth doing
on Matryoshka Language Model Suites
by EylonKrause · October 24, 2026 · edit this page

The paper (Godey & Artzi, 2026) is really about cost: 36% less training compute and 14–26% faster speculative decoding, at near-parity quality. Each number comes from one setup: one training budget, one draft–verifier pair, one baseline, one distillation loss, and joint training throughout. Each follow-up below changes one of these. The question throughout is whether nesting is the cheapest way to make and serve the dolls. (An exit is one nested sub-model, read off at its own LM head.) The follow-ups go roughly from cheapest to most expensive.

1. Does parity hold past 35B tokens?

Why this part: the 36% only matters if quality holds where small models are actually trained, and the paper stops at 35B tokens.

Question. The paper’s curves (Figures 3 and 4) track the gap to vanilla up to 35B tokens, about 11 per parameter for the 3B, below the compute-optimal ~20. SmolLM2’s 1.7B saw about 11T (Allal et al., 2025). With more training, does the gap grow, because one 500M block serves three models, or shrink, because the free distillation keeps paying off?

Experiment. The authors’ Hugging Face page has checkpoints up to 60B tokens with vanilla counterparts, plus an unpublished four-exit 100M / 300M / 600M / 1B suite trained to 105B. Evaluate every exit at every checkpoint against its vanilla counterpart. The cleanest pairs are the 500M exit (up to 60B tokens) and the four-exit suite’s 100M (up to 105B, about 900 tokens per parameter). Mid-run checkpoints never had their learning rate decayed, so branch short matched cooldowns at two or three points, as the paper did for its 23B baseline.

Why it’s worth doing. It is cheap, and it tells #3 and #5 which regime to worry about. It is also the weakest here: the range is still short (the 500M reaches ~120 tokens per parameter, about 50× below SmolLM2), and the authors will probably publish this curve themselves, so share it with them.

2. Every doll as a draft, at a fixed batch size

Why this part: speculative decoding is the paper’s whole inference claim, and it is measured on one pair under conditions that mix several effects.

Question. The paper runs 500M → 3B with each suite at its largest batch: 102 for Matryoshka, 64 for vanilla. So the +26% (greedy) and +14% (nucleus) mix three effects:

  • higher acceptance (+5% accepted length, nucleus only);
  • cheaper verification (the verifier reuses the draft’s 24 layers, roughly 15–20% less compute than a vanilla 3B);
  • a bigger batch.

The suite is also a ladder of drafts, each one’s KV cache the bottom of the next one’s, and the 1.5B–3B pair has the largest agreement gain (+5.7%). Yet only one pair is tested. Does 500M → 1.5B → 3B beat it?

Experiment. Write a loop with one KV cache per block; the released code has no generation.

  • Chains: 500M → 3B, 1.5B → 3B, 500M → 1.5B → 3B, and n-gram → 500M → 1.5B → 3B.
  • Also test small vanilla drafts for the vanilla 3B (vanilla 100M, SmolLM2-135M/360M).
  • Batch sizes 1, 64 for both suites, and the maximum.

Why it’s worth doing. Multi-level speculation exists, even with shared KV caches: HiSpec gets 1.28× over a single level (Kumar et al., 2026). But its middle levels are early exits of one fixed-width model. Here each level is a full model of its own width, and an extra level costs only its LM head (~0.23 GB). The 3B’s last five layers are about half its compute, and a cascade runs them only on tokens the 1.5B accepted. If cascades win, nested suites get multi-level speculation almost free. If not, the fixed-batch runs still split the speedup into its three parts. One caveat: no released model uses grouped-query attention, though Llama-3.2-3B, whose shape the vanilla 3B copies, does. So the memory gain is probably inflated.

3. Compare against train-big-then-prune

Why this part: the 36% is measured against the most expensive way to build a suite, not the one people use.

Question. The baseline trains every size from scratch without distillation, although the paper itself says suite members are “often distilled from larger ones”. Minitron derives 8B and 4B models from a 15B “with up to 40x fewer training tokens per model” (Muralidharan et al., 2024). Llama 3.2 1B and 3B were pruned from Llama 3.1 8B and trained on logits from the 8B and 70B. The paper calls this “complementary” because it starts from “a sunk pretraining cost”, but in a suite the largest model gets trained anyway.

The budget. In \(6ND\) terms (parameters times tokens), a nested suite costs more than its largest model alone, because each exit has its own LM head: about 27% more at 200M scale and 5% more at 3B. Before it costs more than nesting, prune-and-distill can therefore spend about 4–6B distillation tokens per student at 200M scale (20–30% of pretraining), but only 1–2B at 3B (2–6%). Minitron used under 3%. So it fits easily at small scale and tightly at 3B; the question is quality.

Experiment. Prune the released vanilla 3B to the 1.5B and 500M shapes, distill each on 1–2B tokens, and compare with Table 2; pilot it at 200M scale first. Keep one ledger, in \(6ND\) and in wall-clock. They disagree: by \(6ND\) the 3B saving is 35%, but the 200M-scale “41%” is about 27%, probably because the paper converts GPU-time into FLOPs.

Why it’s worth doing. It decides whether the headline means “cheaper than the most expensive way” or “the cheapest way”, and the outcome is open. Cutting 3B to 500M removes 84% of the parameters, which may be too much for pruning to keep its head start. But distillation wins, up to a compute level, “in settings involving many students or an existing teacher” (Busbridge et al., 2025), and that describes a suite. If prune-and-distill wins, nesting’s case narrows to what pruning can’t give: a draft that shares the verifier’s weights and cache.

4. Train the draft for acceptance, not likelihood

Why this part: in a nested suite the draft’s loss is a free choice, and the paper picked it for perplexity, not speed.

Question. A drafted token is accepted with probability \(\beta = \sum_v \min(p_v, q_v)\), one minus the total-variation distance between draft and verifier (Leviathan et al., 2023). For a fixed draft cost, nothing else about the draft matters. The paper trains it on 0.7 next-token loss plus 0.3 cross-entropy to the 3B, tuned for perplexity. Small drafts typically stop short of the shared optimum, and there “minimizing KL does not guarantee maximizing acceptance rate” (Samarin et al., 2026). How much does that cost?

Experiment.

  • No training: on the released \(\alpha_d \in \{0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}\) branches, measure \(\beta\) between the 50M and 200M exits at the paper’s sampling settings; the paper reports only KL and perplexity.
  • At 3B: include the undocumented vanilla 500M_35B_distill. If it is what its name suggests, it separates distillation from weight sharing.
  • Then train: swap in reverse KL, Jensen–Shannon and an acceptance loss, plus an on-policy variant, as short cooldown branches. Train the best one into the head only and into the whole shared block.

Why it’s worth doing. Acceptance training usually freezes the verifier; one unrefereed preprint tunes an 8B target with its draft head. Here the draft is the verifier’s first block, trained from scratch, so report the verifier’s perplexity next to every speedup. The upside is capped: LK losses report up to 8–10% longer accepted length, against the paper’s total 5% gain. A flat result still says agreement is limited by the 1:6 size gap, not the loss.

5. Does the big doll need to teach the small ones?

Why this part: joint end-to-end training is the paper’s central design choice, and the one it never ablates.

Question. In joint training, the 1.5B and 3B losses push gradients into the 500M block. Help or tax? There are hints that the biggest exit pays: at 200M scale the 200M exit trails vanilla (15.62 vs 15.21 perplexity) while the 100M beats it.

Experiment. At 200M scale, with the same tokens and the paper’s loss weights (it blames part of the gap on them, Appendix G):

  • (A) Joint: the paper’s recipe (released).
  • (B) Stop-gradient at every junction: each block learns only from its own exit, while all blocks still train together.
  • (C) Sequential growth: train the 50M (the released vanilla 50M has the same shape), freeze it, add the 100M, freeze, then add the 200M.
  • (D) (C) plus a short joint fine-tune.

Why it’s worth doing. If (B) matches (A), the downward gradient isn’t needed. If (C) matches too, suites can grow after release: add a bigger doll on frozen ones, pay for one block, and every existing exit stays bit-for-bit the same. Nothing I found does this. Greedy local learning isolates blocks but discards their heads (Moon et al., 2026), and LLaMA Pro grows a model around frozen blocks, but the old model is not an exit of the new one (Wu et al., 2024). If they fail, the paper gains its most important missing ablation.
